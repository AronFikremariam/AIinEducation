Why this matters now

AI tools can explain a hard concept, draft an outline, or translate a reading. They can also store what you type, repeat confident mistakes, and blur the line between your work and a machine’s.

Using them well starts with knowing three things: where your information goes, which rules apply to you, and what is fair to ask a tool to do.

  • Your inputs may be stored.Many AI services keep prompts, and some may use them to improve their models, depending on the settings and plan. Check each tool’s policy.
  • Answers can be wrong.AI can state false information fluently, including invented sources. Verify anything that matters.
  • Rules vary by school and place.Course policies, privacy laws, and tool terms of service all differ. When unsure, ask.

Three big questions

Pick one to explore. Each covers what can go wrong and what to do about it.

Is my information safe?

  • Data leakage: anything typed into a public AI tool leaves your control. Keep personal, financial, and confidential details out of prompts.
  • Account security: use a unique password and multi-factor authentication on every AI account.
  • Prompt injection: text hidden in a web page or document can try to steer an AI tool that reads it. Be careful when asking AI to process content you don’t trust.
  • Phishing and deepfakes: AI makes convincing fake emails, voices, and images easier to produce. Verify unexpected requests through a second channel.
  • Extensions and plug-ins: third-party add-ons may request broad access to your data. Install only what you need from sources you trust.

For institutions: review vendors before adoption, limit who can access what, and ask where data is stored, how long it is kept, and whether it is used for model training.

Which rules apply?

  • Student privacy: in the United States, FERPA protects student education records. Schools generally cannot share identifiable records with a tool or vendor without proper authority or agreements.
  • Children’s data: COPPA limits how online services collect personal information from children under 13. Many AI tools also set their own minimum ages.
  • International privacy law: the EU’s GDPR and similar laws elsewhere govern how personal data is collected, used, and kept, and can apply to institutions or users abroad.
  • Copyright and ownership: who owns AI-assisted work, and whether training on existing works is allowed, is still being decided in courts and legislatures. Read each tool’s terms and cite your use.
  • Academic integrity: your course and school policies decide what AI help is allowed. Rules can differ class by class.
  • Accessibility: institutions may have legal duties to make technology usable for people with disabilities, so tools should be checked for accessibility.

This site is informational and is not legal advice. Laws change and depend on where you live. Check official sources or ask your school’s legal or compliance office.

What is fair and honest?

  • Be transparent: say when and how you used AI, in the way your instructor or institution asks.
  • Keep thinking: use AI to support learning, not replace it. If a tool does the reasoning, you miss the practice.
  • Watch for bias: AI reflects patterns in its training data and can treat people or perspectives unfairly. Question one-sided or stereotyped outputs.
  • Check accuracy: confirm facts, quotes, and citations against reliable sources before relying on them.
  • Think about access: not every student has the same tools, devices, or support. Policies should avoid giving some students an unfair advantage or disadvantage.
  • Keep a human in the loop: decisions about grades, discipline, or admissions should not be left to AI alone.

A good test: could you explain to your instructor, in your own words, what you submitted and how you made it?

Before you paste: a quick data check

Open each example to see how careful to be. The colored dot shows the level: stop, take care, or generally fine.

A classmate’s name and grades Don’t paste

Identifiable student information about other people is private and may be protected by law. Remove names and details, or don’t share it at all.

Passwords, ID numbers, or bank details Don’t paste

No AI task needs these. Keeping them out of prompts avoids exposing them if a service stores or leaks data.

Your own essay draft Take care

Check your course policy first. Remove personal details, and know whether the tool keeps or reuses what you submit.

A work or school document marked internal Take care

Confidential material usually needs approval. Ask whether your organization has an approved AI tool for this.

A general question like “Explain photosynthesis” Generally fine

No personal or sensitive data is involved. Still verify the answer against your textbook or another reliable source.

Public information with no personal details Generally fine

Public text is lower risk, but copyright still applies, and you should still check what the tool says back.

Your responsible-use guide

Choose who the guide is for.

Five habits for students

  1. Read your course’s AI policy before you start, and ask if it’s unclear.
  2. Keep private details out of prompts: yours and other people’s.
  3. Treat answers as drafts. Check facts and sources yourself.
  4. Do the thinking. Use AI to explain, quiz, or give feedback, not to do the assignment for you.
  5. Be open about your use and keep notes on how you used it.

Six steps for schools and colleges

  1. Write a clear, plain-language AI policy and update it on a schedule.
  2. Review vendors for security, privacy terms, data retention, and accessibility before approval.
  3. Provide approved tools so students and staff aren’t pushed toward unvetted ones.
  4. Train students and staff on privacy, accuracy, and academic integrity.
  5. Keep human review in any decision that affects a student’s record or future.
  6. Collect feedback, watch for unequal access, and adjust.

Key terms

Generative AI
Software that produces new text, images, audio, or code from a prompt.
Prompt
The instruction or question you give an AI tool.
Hallucination
A confident answer that is false or made up, such as a fake citation.
Training data
The material a model learned from. It shapes what the model knows and where its biases come from.
Personal data
Information that can identify a person, such as a name, ID number, or grades.
Multi-factor authentication
A second proof of identity, like a code on your phone, in addition to a password.
Algorithmic bias
Systematic unfairness in how a system treats people or groups.
